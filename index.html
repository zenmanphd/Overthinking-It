Overthinking it

'Tell me, what is a good place to eat?' What a harmless, new kid in town question to ask around the coffee maker. Most, if not all Richmond locals are happy, if not proud to recommend a handful of diners, dives, and delicatessens from their mental rolodex of ‘places people seem to like.’ Of course, where you like to eat can be very personal, and for many in the river city I might as well be asking for your Netflix password. I am willing to bet that we have all been restaurant-shamed at some point. "Why do you go there?" Your friend or cursed rival might ask before name dropping a 'much better place.' Don't take it to heart. That interaction is as quintessential an aspect of our regional personality as our obsession with brunch and exposed brick. For something to be your favorite thing, it doesn’t have to be the best, and if we’re honest with ourselves, it often isn’t. Now that the preamble is out of the way, I miss Sticky Rice. I don’t just mean the restaurant and hipster frequented, sushi-themed dive bar. It is still alive and tater-totting on Alison and Main Street. I miss the idea of Sticky Rice, the ‘vibe’ as the kids say. There's a place that comes to mind immediately when you want to catch up with a friend. Sometimes you don't want to think too hard when it comes to eating out; you don’t want to weigh the unknown variables of parking or prices. For me (whose bachelor pad was barely 4 blocks away and 6 while intoxicated) Sticky Rice was a place I knew so well that looking to see what was new on the menu barely entered my mind, because even the food was just a part of the scenery. I don't really make it out there anymore. I've found new dives; many are closer to me, but I didn't completely abandon Sticky Rice either. It lives in this record store of nostalgia in my mind, kept safe in its vintage plastic sleeve. So, I guess we're talking about “dives,” but in the best possible terms. A good dive has many uses. You may try a meal special at a restaurant, but you meet your soulmate at a dive. Restaurants come and go with the tides of trends and tastes. A dive is a part of the neighborhood. It shares DNA with the library and the post office; the sidewalk, buckled by tree roots. That beer stained, oak table dimension that transforms cities into small towns one happy hour at a time is like another neighbor. Places like this had a different meaning for me when I was younger. The first time getting to drive without supervision, sub shops, bakeries, and markets were checkpoints of independence. The places of simple pleasure between school and home that I chose to go were GPS points along the route forming my pre-adult identity. There are hundreds of places in the city to buy sandwiches. Why I chose the spots I did aside from location was the result of an aspect of my preferences and tastes. “This deli is nice” I’d think, but also that deli was me. Every corner of this city is someone’s corner, someone’s part of town, whether they live there or not. A band’s hit song may lure you in initially, but often it’s not until you comb through the B-sides that you find ‘your song.’ Cities are like this. If you stay on the main business route you will find Walmart and Cosco and Burger King and if you woke up from a coma in either parking lot you wouldn’t know which city you were in. What’s great about a city is finding all those side streets of local personality that let you know you’re home and nowhere else. These are the things that create a sense of place. When you free yourself from the Yelp review guardrails and follow your own curiosity, it can be rewarding. There's this place I like to go for Philly Cheese Steaks. The subs are filled to the brim with meaty, cheesy vice; it's substantial, rolled up tight in foil and deli paper like a gift. Hot grease and steam break down the brown paper bag before you make it to your car, and even that is part of it. It is just a modest deli on the side of the road, joined at the hip with a vape shop; it is almost generous in its lack of flair. Outside there are paint chipped antique, Grandma's garden-style cast metal tables and chairs overlooking the cracked asphalt of a sunbaked Lakeside Avenue. I love everything about it. I bring my wife there and we eat outside. "I can see why you like this place, it’s you," she says. I know what she means.
